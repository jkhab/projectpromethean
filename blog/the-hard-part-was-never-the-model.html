The Hard Part Was Never the Model

Jun 14, 2026 · 4 min read

I directed and shipped a paid recovery app to the App Store, and the model was the easy part. Most of the real work was deciding what it's allowed to say and when it has to hand off to a person.

For years I had a list of things I wanted to build and no way to build them. I never learned to code, so the list sat there. Then AI took the excuse away. Work the Steps is live on the App Store now, people pay for it, and I designed every behavior in it without writing a line of the code myself. I expected that last part to be the story. It turned out the code was never the hard part.

A demo just has to talk. Production has to do the right thing when a real person and a real risk show up at the same time, and the model doesn't help you there. Those are judgment calls, and making them was most of what building this actually was.

The two messages that look identical

Someone working their fourth step writes about a past night: a bottle of pills out on the counter, how close it got. Someone else types, tonight, "I have the pills here now, I don't think I can do tonight." The same words trip the same keyword trigger. One is an honest accounting of a night they survived; the other is happening right now.

A system that alarms at both teaches people to hide. The fourth step only works if someone can write the worst of it down honestly, and if the app treats that honesty like an emergency, they'll stop. So the design reads present danger against recounted history and lets tense and intent decide the response, since a keyword can't tell those two people apart. That decision didn't come from anything technical. It came from knowing how recovery works.

What it says when it can't fix it

For the live crisis, the app tells the truth about itself. It says, plainly, "I'm an app, I can't keep you safe tonight, but a person can," then makes one concrete ask and points to a real human. Pretending it could hold someone through that night would be a lie, and the person on the other end would feel it. Admitting what it is buys enough trust for the handoff to land.

The screen with the real numbers sits one tap from anywhere in the app, with no AI in the path. The one thing that has to work can't depend on the model working.

The voice is a design decision

Voice got the same treatment. Trust breaks fast when an AI sounds like an AI, so I keep a running list of the tells and of the therapy-speak that performs care instead of giving it. The app doesn't say "I hear you," and it doesn't open a reply with "thank you for sharing." I can name those phrases because I've heard the hollow version of every one of them.

Safety works the same way. The response to a crisis word can't depend on the model noticing a rule buried somewhere in a long set of instructions, so the right directive gets put in front of it every time the situation calls for one.

The point

None of this is specific to recovery. Any conversation with real weight behind it has the same shape: the model can talk on day one, and whether it does the right thing when stakes show up is a separate question nobody answers for you. It's the same work in a support queue, just with money on the line instead.

I built the full breakdown as something you can step through turn by turn, including how the present-versus-recounted call plays out in a real exchange: Designing how an AI behaves under real stakes.

The models keep getting better on their own. Deciding what to let one do hasn't gotten any easier, and that's the part of the work I like.
