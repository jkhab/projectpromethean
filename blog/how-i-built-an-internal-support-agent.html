I Built a Support Agent That Reads Your Help Center and Learns

Jun 14, 2026 · 8 min read

You load your help center into it once. It answers customer questions from those docs, with citations, and when the docs come up short it says so instead of guessing. Answers a person approves get remembered, so the same question is never hard twice. It runs on a laptop, and below it's answering real questions on a real help center you can go check yourself.

For most of ten years in support, I was a lookup table with a pulse. The same dozen questions over and over, plus a handful only I could answer because the answer lived in my head and nowhere else. New hires waited on me for it, customers waited on the queue, and the stuff the senior people knew, the most useful knowledge in the building, stayed the hardest to get at.

The fix I eventually built at work wasn't a customer-facing chatbot. It was an assistant for the team: load the knowledge in once and stop being the bottleneck. I can't show you that one, so I rebuilt the idea from scratch in the open.

One thing first: this isn't advice on which support AI to buy. I build the thing and show you the parts that don't demo well.

What it does, in one breath

Load your help center once and ask it a customer's question. It drafts an answer using only your own articles, shows which ones it used, and hands the draft to a person to review and send. If your docs don't cover the question, it says so and routes to a human instead of inventing something. When a person approves an answer, it saves it, so next time it already knows.

Watch it work, on a real help center

To make this checkable, I pointed it at Medium's public help center, about two hundred articles, and asked it real questions. The screens below are its real answers, shown the way they appear in the tool. You can also click through them in a live, no-key preview.

That answer is right, and every fact in it traces to a named article a reviewer can open. The $10 minimum and the Stripe detail didn't come out of thin air; they came out of Medium's own docs.

Support Answer Assistant
Question: Can I connect Medium to my company's Salesforce CRM?
Not covered — needs a human
I'm sorry, but the help center and approved answers don't cover connecting Medium to Salesforce or any CRM, so I can't advise on that. I'd recommend having a human teammate follow up on this one.
No sources to stand on, so it gets out of the way instead of bluffing.

This is the part most demos skip. An ungrounded bot answers this question confidently and wrongly. This one looked, found nothing it could stand on, and got out of the way. That behavior is what decides whether a team keeps using the tool, because a bot that bluffs only has to burn them once or twice before they quietly stop.

The part that makes it matter: it learns

That Salesforce question is a gap. So a human answers it once, and approves that answer. Watch what happens the next time someone asks the same thing:

Support Answer Assistant
Question: Can I connect Medium to my company's Salesforce CRM? (asked again, later)
Answered from a learned answer
Medium does not offer a built-in Salesforce or CRM integration. If you need Medium data in a CRM, you can export your stats from the Stats page and import them, or use a third-party automation tool to move the data over.

It went from "I don't know, escalate" to a clean, instant answer because a person taught it once. Every escalation today becomes tomorrow's self-service; that's the engine. It's the stop answering the same question twice idea in practice.

How it actually works

Three moving parts, and the model is the least interesting one.

It loads your whole help center once and keeps it. You point it at your help center, it reads every published article in one pass, and it holds onto them. After that you just ask; there's no re-importing or feeding it articles per question.

The instructions do the real work. When a question comes in, the model gets your articles, your team's approved answers, and a short set of rules. Those rules are the product:

Answer using ONLY the approved answers and help-center articles
provided. Do not use outside knowledge.

If a team-approved answer covers it, prefer it.
Otherwise cite the help-center article(s) you used.

If neither covers the question, say so plainly and recommend a
human take it. Do not guess, and do not stitch together a
plausible answer the sources don't support.

Never invent steps, prices, URLs, or policies.

The "only the sources" rule stops it answering from the model's general memory, which sounds fine and is sometimes quietly wrong about your product. Citing what it used means a human can verify in seconds. And "I don't know" counts as a correct answer, which is where the escalation behavior above comes from.

A person stays between the draft and the customer. The output is a draft; someone reads it, edits if needed, and sends it. A reply from the bot only counts as a resolution once the customer actually got what they came for, and keeping a human in the loop protects that while the system earns its keep. I went deeper on measuring that gap here.

How it helps, concretely

The new hire on a Tuesday night gets the senior answer without waiting for the senior person, and the expert answers each new thing once instead of fifty times. Questions nobody ever wrote down get written down the moment they're first asked. And because every answer shows its sources and passes through a person, the team comes to trust it instead of working around it. On a small team, that's the difference between drowning as volume grows and keeping up with it.

Using it day to day

It runs as an app on your own computer. Load your help center once. Type a question, read the draft, check the sources, edit if needed, send. Click approve to teach it. That's the loop. Your knowledge and your API key stay on your machine; nothing is uploaded.

The honest limits

It's only as good as the docs behind it. A confident answer built on a stale article is worse than no answer, which is why step zero is auditing the help center before the AI gets anywhere near it. I wrote about that here, and the tool I use to find the rot is here. It also has no memory of the specific customer, and the per-answer cost is small but real, so in front of a team you'd put a hard spending cap and a kill switch on it, lessons I paid for elsewhere.

The point

The whole thing is about a hundred and fifty lines and runs on a laptop with no setup beyond an API key. Almost none of the work was the model. It was judgment about what to ground it on, when it should answer, and when it should get out of the way, and ten years in the queue is where that came from. This is what it looks like built.
