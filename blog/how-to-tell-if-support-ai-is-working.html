How to Tell If Your Support AI Is Actually Working

May 26, 2026 · 5 min read

A resolved ticket means somebody took an action. Whether the customer got what they came for is a separate question, and most support AI gets judged without anyone asking it.

Most support AI gets judged on the wrong moment. A ticket comes in, someone or something takes an action, the ticket closes, and a number goes up: resolved, contained, deflected. The number is real, but it only measures whether you acted. Whether the customer ended up with what they came for is a different question.

I've spent a decade in enterprise support and I build with AI tools, so I'm not skeptical of it. Used well it's the strongest lever the function has. It's also easy to roll out in a way that lifts the dashboard while the customer's week stays exactly as annoying as it was, and the numbers won't warn you when that happens. Here's how I tell the difference.

The number that lies

Resolution marks the moment you finished an action. The outcome can show up much later. Two cases show how far apart they sit.

The first is the customer who gave up. The AI made it hard enough to reach a person that they stopped trying. No new ticket, no escalation, so the interaction reads as contained, and the dashboard sees success. From the top, abandonment and containment look identical.

The second is the action that didn't land. Take a routine request: connect our new product to the data feed. You connect it, close the ticket, resolved. Whether data actually flows doesn't become clear for a month, and there's no built-in way to circle back, so nobody does. A month later the customer files a new ticket: still not connected. The first ticket got marked resolved because the action was taken. The second ticket is the real verdict on the first.

Why leadership never sees it

Nothing in the default reporting links that second ticket back to the first. The platform can relate a follow-up to its parent and flag a reopen, but that linkage almost never gets set up, so it never reaches the number leadership reads. The reopened ticket shows up as fresh work and gets counted as fresh work. Nobody hides the failure. It just never gets connected, so the headline number stays clean.

What to measure instead

If resolution on the day of action is the lie, the honest version measures the outcome over the time the outcome takes to appear. A few things I'd put on the scorecard.

  • Measure repeat contact by account, not by ticket. Matching ticket to ticket by same customer and same subject fails both ways: a brand-new issue looks like a repeat, and a real repeat from a different person or channel at the same account gets missed. Tying return contact to the account survives both.
  • Give each kind of issue its own window. A failed password reset comes back in hours. A data feed comes back in a month. One flat window, like a reopen within seven days, calls the slow-failing issues a success and the fast ones a failure. Bucket the window by issue type.
  • Watch how your best people use the tool. On a tool that drafts replies, the signal is simple: did the agent send the draft as is, tweak it, or throw it out and rewrite. Lots of throwing-out by your most experienced people is worth taking seriously, because they're the ones who can spot a wrong answer on sight.

The metrics are simple. Producing them is the work, because most ticket setups have no field that joins a reopened ticket to the one that caused it.

The AI is only as good as the docs behind it

An AI answer is only as good as your knowledge base. A confident answer built on a stale doc is worse than no answer, because a person skims a stale article and moves on, while the AI reads it straight and ships it as fact. So AI raises the upkeep on your knowledge base rather than lowering it.

The other half is who's holding the tool. The same draft produces opposite outcomes depending on who reads it. An experienced person catches when the AI is confident and wrong and rewrites it. A junior assumes the AI knows more than they do, trusts a confident wrong answer, and ships it to the customer. That puts the risk on your most junior people, who are exactly who the tool gets bought to help. Keep a person between the draft and the send.

One plain rule for the tempting next step: if you let the AI close tickets with no human in the loop, only let it close the kinds of issues where a wrong answer is cheap and easy to catch, and sample what it sends.

The short version

If you're a support leader being pitched AI right now, the test is two questions. How would you know the customer got what they came for, measured over the time it actually takes to find out? And who checks the AI's answer before a customer sees it? A tool that improves those two things is doing the job. If the dashboard is climbing and neither one is being measured, all it's really telling you is how busy the tool has been.
